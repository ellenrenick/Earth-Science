## Station 2.1: How Earth Formed

**Reteach.** About 4.6 billion years ago, a spinning cloud of gas and dust called the **solar nebula** collapsed. The Sun formed in the center. Around it, dust and rock clumped together, crashed and stuck. This growing-by-collision is called **accretion**. It built the planets and left behind leftover chunks called **asteroids**.

- **Big bodies (like Earth)** got hot enough to melt. Heavy iron sank to the center to make a core, and lighter rock rose. Earth's materials were mixed and recycled.
- **Small bodies (asteroids)** cooled fast. Many never melted, so they still hold the solar system's original material, like a time capsule.

**Worked example.** *Why do iron meteorites matter?* They are pieces of the metal cores of broken asteroids. That shows that bodies which melted could form iron cores, which is evidence that Earth formed its iron core the same way.

## Station 2.2: Radioactive Clocks

**Reteach.** Some atoms are unstable. A **parent** atom decays into a **daughter** atom (uranium-238 → lead-206). The **half-life** is the time for half the parent atoms to decay. Heat, pressure and chemistry do **not** change it. That steady rate is what makes it a clock.

| Half-lives passed | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Parent left | 100% | 50% | 25% | 12.5% | 6.25% |

**Three steps to a rock's age:** (1) find the % of parent left, (2) count how many half-lives that is, (3) multiply by the length of one half-life.

**Worked example.** A rock has 12.5% of its potassium-40 left (half-life 1.25 billion years). 100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5 is **3 half-lives**. 3 × 1.25 = **3.75 billion years old**.

**Pick the right clock:** uranium-238 (4.47 billion yrs) for the oldest rocks and meteorites · potassium-40 (1.25 billion yrs) for volcanic rocks · carbon-14 (5,730 yrs) only for once-living things up to about 50,000 years old.

## Station 2.3: Why Earth's Oldest Rocks Are Gone

**Reteach.** Earth is about 4.54 billion years old, but its oldest whole rock (the Acasta Gneiss) is only about 4.0 billion years old. That 500-million-year gap is not because there were no rocks. Earth is an active planet, and **surface processes** keep destroying old rock:

- **Weathering** breaks rock into small pieces.
- **Erosion** carries those pieces away (water, wind, ice).
- **Volcanism** buries old surfaces under new lava.
- **Plate tectonics** pushes old crust back into the mantle, where it melts.

A few tiny, tough **zircon** crystals (about 4.4 billion years old) survived inside younger rock after the rock they formed in wore away.

**Worked example.** *Claim:* "Earth has no rocks older than 4.0 billion years, so nothing happened before then." *Fix:* Things did happen; the rocks were recycled. Zircons from 4.4 billion years ago prove solid crust existed earlier.

## Station 2.4: Evidence for Earth's Age

**Reteach.** We can't date Earth's oldest rocks because they're gone. So scientists use objects that formed at the **same time, from the same cloud** and were never recycled.

| Object | Age (billion years) | What it tells us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chondrite meteorites | \~4.56 | Oldest dates in the solar system; composition matches the Sun minus gases, so they show Earth's building blocks |
| Apollo Moon rocks | \~4.4–4.5 | The Moon formed soon after Earth; rocks are similar to Earth's mantle |
| Jack Hills zircons | \~4.4 | Earth had solid crust (and likely water) very early |
| Acasta Gneiss | \~4.0 | Oldest whole Earth rock, so most older rock was destroyed |

**Worked example.** *Why does a meteorite's age tell us Earth's age?* The Sun, planets and asteroids formed from the same cloud at nearly the same time. Meteorites were never melted or recycled, so radiometric dating gives their true age, about 4.56 billion years. That's our best estimate for Earth too.

## Station 2.5: Craters as Evidence

**Reteach.** An **impact crater** is a bowl-shaped hole made when an object from space hits a surface. Early in the solar system, Earth and the Moon were both hit many, many times.

- **Craters pile up over time.** If two surfaces are hit at the same rate, the surface with **more craters is older**: it has been sitting there collecting hits longer.
- **The Moon keeps its craters** because it has almost no weathering, erosion, volcanism today or plate tectonics. It has countless craters.
- **Earth erases its craters** with surface processes. It has fewer than 200 confirmed craters.

So the Moon is a record of the early bombardment that Earth went through too.

**Worked example.** The Moon's bright highlands are covered in overlapping craters and have rocks \~4.4–4.5 billion years old. The dark maria have far fewer craters and rocks \~3.2–3.9 billion years old. More craters plus older rock ages means the **highlands are the older surface**.

## Watch out: common mix-ups

- **"The oldest Earth rock is Earth's age."** No. Earth's oldest rocks were recycled. Earth's age comes from meteorites (\~4.56 billion years).
- **"Heat speeds up radioactive decay."** No. The half-life never changes, which is why it works as a clock.
- **"After 2 half-lives, all of it is gone."** No. Each half-life cuts what's left in half: 100% → 50% → 25%.
- **"The Moon has more craters because more things hit it."** No. Earth was hit too; its surface processes erased the craters.
- **"Meteorite, meteor, meteoroid are the same."** Meteoroid = in space. Meteor = the streak of light. Meteorite = the piece that lands.
