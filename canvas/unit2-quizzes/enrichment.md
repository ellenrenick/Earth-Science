## Challenge A: The Lead Detective

**Read.** In the early 1950s, a young geochemist named **Clair Patterson** set out to measure Earth's age. He knew Earth's oldest rocks had been recycled, so he turned to a **meteorite**: the Canyon Diablo meteorite, pieces of the iron meteorite that made Meteor Crater in Arizona. Uranium decays into lead, so the mix of lead types in the meteorite works as a clock. In 1956 he published an age of about **4.55 billion years**, very close to today's best estimate.

There was a problem along the way: his measurements kept coming out wrong because there was lead *everywhere*: in the dust, the air, even his lab glassware. He built one of the first ultra-clean labs to get rid of it. While tracking down the extra lead, he found that most of it came from **leaded gasoline**. His work helped lead to leaded gas being phased out in the United States.

**Questions**

1. Why did Patterson date a meteorite instead of an Earth rock? Use the words *meteorite* and *surface processes*.
2. Lead is the **daughter** in uranium decay. If extra lead from the air got into his sample, would the meteorite seem **older or younger** than it really is? Explain.
3. Patterson set out to answer one question (Earth's age) and ended up answering another (lead pollution). Describe a time when solving one problem led you to a new question.

## Challenge C: Crater Census

Every rocky surface in the inner solar system has been hit by space objects. How many craters a surface still has depends on its age and on what **surface processes** erase them.

| Body | What its surface looks like | Surface processes today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Moon | Highlands packed with overlapping craters; maria have fewer | Almost none (no air, no water, no plate tectonics) |
| Mercury | Heavily cratered almost everywhere | Almost none |
| Mars | Southern highlands heavily cratered; northern plains have far fewer | Wind erosion today; flowing water and volcanoes in the past; no plate tectonics |
| Venus | Only about 1,000 craters, spread evenly across the planet | Thick atmosphere; huge volcanic eruptions repaved the surface |
| Earth | Fewer than 200 confirmed craters | Weathering, erosion, volcanism, plate tectonics, oceans |

**Questions**

1. Rank the five bodies from the **oldest average surface** to the **youngest**. Explain your top and bottom picks.
2. Mars has very old, heavily cratered land in the south and smoother, less cratered land in the north. What does that tell you about Mars' history?
3. Venus has no plate tectonics, but scientists think its whole surface is much younger than the Moon's. What surface process could explain that?
4. If you wanted to study what the solar system was like 4.4 billion years ago, which body would you send a rover to, and why?

## Challenge D: Where Did the Moon Come From?

**The hypothesis.** The leading idea, the **giant-impact hypothesis**, says that about 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized body crashed into the young Earth. Melted rock and debris from both bodies were blasted into orbit, then clumped together (accretion again!) to form the Moon.

**Evidence cards**

- **Card 1:** Moon rocks are about 4.4–4.5 billion years old, only slightly younger than meteorites (\~4.56).
- **Card 2:** Moon rocks have a **composition** very similar to Earth's mantle. Some chemical "fingerprints" in them are nearly identical to Earth's.
- **Card 3:** The Moon has only a small iron core compared to Earth.
- **Card 4:** Moon rocks show signs that the Moon's outer layer was once a deep ocean of melted rock.

**Write a CER (8–10 sentences):** *Does the evidence support the giant-impact hypothesis?*

- Use at least 3 evidence cards and explain how each one fits (or doesn't fit) the hypothesis.
- Hint for Card 3: if the impact mostly blasted off Earth's outer rocky layers, what would be missing from the Moon?
- End with one **question that still needs answering** or one piece of evidence you'd want scientists to collect next.
